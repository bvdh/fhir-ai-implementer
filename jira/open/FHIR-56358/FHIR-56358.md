For clinicians interested in *adverse drug events (ADEs)* and *pharmacovigilance, **Mini-Sentinel* and *Sentinel* refer to FDA-sponsored active surveillance systems designed to detect drug safety problems using large-scale healthcare data rather than relying solely on voluntary adverse event reports. [[fda.gov]](https://www.fda.gov/safety/fdas-sentinel-initiative), [[fda.gov]](https://www.fda.gov/safety/fdas-sentinel-initiative/fdas-sentinel-initiative-background)

Before Sentinel: Passive Surveillance

Traditionally, the FDA relied heavily on the *FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS)*, which collects voluntary reports of suspected adverse drug events from clinicians, manufacturers, and patients. These reports are valuable for signal generation but have limitations:

Underreporting
Reporting bias
Lack of denominator data (how many patients took the drug)
Difficulty estimating actual risk

Mini-Sentinel (2008–2016)

The *Mini-Sentinel Pilot* was the first phase of the FDA's Sentinel Initiative. It was launched in 2008 to determine whether a national electronic post-market drug safety surveillance system was feasible. The pilot informed development of the full Sentinel System. [[fda.gov]](https://www.fda.gov/safety/fdas-sentinel-initiative), [[fda.gov]](https://www.fda.gov/safety/fdas-sentinel-initiative/fdas-sentinel-initiative-background)

Key features:

Used large administrative claims databases and electronic health records.
Employed a *distributed data network*, meaning patient-level data remained with participating health systems and insurers.
Allowed FDA investigators to run standardized analyses across multiple organizations without centralizing identifiable patient data. [[sentinelin...iative.org]](https://www.sentinelinitiative.org/), [[fda.gov]](https://www.fda.gov/safety/fdas-sentinel-initiative/fdas-sentinel-initiative-background)

Sentinel (2016–Present)

The full *Sentinel System* officially launched in 2016 after the Mini-Sentinel pilot phase. It is now one of the world's largest distributed databases dedicated to medical product safety surveillance. [[fda.gov]](https://www.fda.gov/safety/fdas-sentinel-initiative), [[fda.gov]](https://www.fda.gov/safety/fdas-sentinel-initiative/fdas-sentinel-initiative-background)

Sentinel supports:

Active surveillance of approved drugs
Vaccine safety studies
Biologics and medical device monitoring
Rapid assessment of emerging safety signals
Real-world evidence generation for regulatory decision-making [[fda.gov]](https://www.fda.gov/safety/fdas-sentinel-initiative), [[sentinelin...iative.org]](https://www.sentinelinitiative.org/studies/drugs)

Why Clinicians Should Care

Sentinel enables questions that cannot easily be answered through spontaneous reporting systems:

*Example ADE questions:*

Does semaglutide increase the risk of a rare complication?
Is Drug A associated with more acute kidney injury than Drug B?
What is the incidence of severe hypoglycemia after initiating a medication?
Are observed adverse events occurring more often than expected?

Using claims and EHR data, Sentinel can compare exposed and unexposed populations and estimate relative risks in real-world clinical practice. [[sentinelin...iative.org]](https://www.sentinelinitiative.org/studies/drugs), [[sentinelin...iative.org]](https://www.sentinelinitiative.org/)

Active Risk Identification and Analysis (ARIA)

Within Sentinel, the major drug safety surveillance component is called *ARIA (Active Risk Identification and Analysis)*. FDA uses ARIA to:

Assess known serious risks
Evaluate newly detected safety signals
Investigate unexpected serious risks after drug approval

These studies can contribute to label changes, FDA safety communications, and other regulatory actions. [[sentinelin...iative.org]](https://www.sentinelinitiative.org/studies/drugs)

Clinical Informatics Perspective

As a physician informaticist, you might think of Sentinel as an early national-scale implementation of what we now call *real-world evidence (RWE)*:

FHIR Connection

Although Sentinel historically relied on claims and EHR data mapped to a common data model rather than FHIR, many informaticians view Sentinel as a precursor to modern learning health systems. A future architecture could increasingly leverage FHIR resources such as:

*MedicationRequest*
*MedicationAdministration*
*MedicationStatement*
*Condition*
*Observation*
*Encounter*
*AdverseEvent*

to support near-real-time pharmacovigilance across healthcare organizations.

One important FHIR nuance, given our earlier discussion: *AdverseEvent* is the FHIR resource that represents an actual adverse occurrence, whereas *AllergyIntolerance* represents a patient's propensity toward a future adverse reaction rather than an event that has already occurred.

### 3. Russell Leftwich - 2026-06-26

Agree with above, except the last sentence: AdverseEvent and AllergyIntolerance. The former is an event, which might be an adverse reaction and might be an allergic reaction. But AllergyIntolerance is the propensity of an individual to a future reaction to a substance.

AdverseEvent in clinical care also implies that there was an abherrent workflow that caused the adverse event. In a clinical trial, the an adverse event is defined in the study protocol, usually as any injury or adverse physiologic occurrence. In clinical care, an adverse event is an assertion of cause and effect. In a clinical trial, there is no assertion about cause. A trial subject who is on placebo can have an adverse event.. Adverse events in clinical trials are captured to distinguish whether subjects on active drugs have more adverse events than those on placebo.

an allergic reaction (which implies an immunologic cause of the reaction, vs an intolerance where the mechanism is non-immunologic) is not an adverse event if the patient never had a reaction to that substance (drug) before. It becomes an advert event if their record shows an allergy/intolerance and they were then given the drug that they were known to react to. A bee sting can cause exactly the same allergic reaction as penicillin, but it is not an adverse event because they were not stung by the healthcare system.

Angioedmema from ACE inhibitors looks indistigushable from angioedema from penicillin, but the mechanism is due to altered bradykinin kallidrein activation and is not immunologic. so, the latter is an intolerance and the propensity is captured in the AllergyIntolerance resource.

### 5. Russell Leftwich - 2026-06-19

Repeated lactic acidosis on metformin is blatant mismanagement of the patient. Nothing more. Unless perhaps the patient took more than the prescribed dose.

Lactic acidosis is an inherent risk of metformin because this medication impairs the clearance of lactate from the body. Because metformin is removed from the body by the kidney, reduced kidney function including the decline in kidney function with age, causes increased metformin in the body and can lead, will lead, to lactic acidosis. Because the liver clears lactate from the body, liver disease also increases the risk of lactic acidosis while on metformin. Acute kidney injury, excessive alcohol use, and hypoxia can also lead to lactic acidosis while on metformin.

This individual with repeated lactic acidosis must have some factor(s) of those listed above and should not have been prescribed metformin a second time, let alone multiple times.

### 6. Jay Lyle - 2026-06-18

The example use case was a patient experiencing multiple episodes of metformin-associated lactic acidosis. This is clearly an adverse drug event, not an allergy.

If the providers are not following the protocol for administering metformin, that may also be a provider error, and this is a very unlucky patient. MALA risk factors that are detectable 'states' include tissue hypoxia, depressed kidney or liver function, & dehydration, among others.

That's a risk factor that can be classified as a 'trait' rather than a 'state' - it persists for that patient, and in this case it's not discoverable in a normal clinical setting. I'm not seeing a lot of evidence that the variation is even a proven cause, or that it's at all common, so perhaps this use case is a bad example.

But the discriminator for what goes in the 'AllergyIntolerance' resource is that there is a persistent characteristic of the patient that puts the patient at risk in the presence of the identified substance.

Which is what the spec already says: "Risk of harmful or undesirable physiological response which is specific to an individual and associated with exposure to a substance."

So perhaps there's no enhancement needed.

### 7. Russell Leftwich - 2026-04-15

Allergy/Intolerance and Adverse Drug Events are different concepts and absolutely should not, cannot be combined.

AllergyIntolerance is a propensity to a future reaction that is unique to an individual. It is not an event.

Adverse Reaction, Adverse Event, and Adverse Drug Event are separate concepts that have different definitions and should not be confused, though they often are. Adverse Event has separate definitions depending on whether the individual is a patient or a subject in a clinical trial.
