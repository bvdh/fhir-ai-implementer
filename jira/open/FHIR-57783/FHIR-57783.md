# FHIR-57783: Support authorizations without request to actions

- **Reporter**: Elliot Silver
- **Resolution**: Unresolved
- **Specification**: FHIR Core (FHIR)
- **Raised in Version**: R6
- **Work Group**: FHIR Infrastructure
- **Related Artifact(s)**: DeviceRequestMedicationRequestServiceRequestVisionPrescriptionEvent Pattern
- **Change Category**: Enhancement
- **Description**: While discussing addition of VisionPrescription.intent (FHIR-55995), it became apparent that there is no code in RequestIntent (http://hl7.org/fhir/request-intent) to indicate a prescription that conveys authorization without a requirement to act.

In most out-patient environments, a prescription given to a patient for a medication, massage therapy, glasses, or home medical equipment only conveys the authorization that such a product may be given to the patient; it is not an order to anyone to do so. Further for requests other than medications, it would be reasonable for single authorization can be actioned multiple times (e.g., use the same vision prescription for multiple sets of glasses).

I can also imaging in an in-patient environment that a medication could be authorized without a direction that it be dispensed/administered – something that could be administered if needed (perhaps at the patient's determination, perhaps with the nursing staff's determination).

Add a new concept to http://hl7.org/fhir/request-intent to indicate authorization without instruction to act. This might be a parent to order, as order is a specialization that adds requirement to act on the base authorization.

## Comments

### 1. Lloyd McKenzie - 2026-07-09

In FHIR terms they are. The "please fulfill" is always separate. It has to be - because the status of the authorization and the status of the fulfillment are distinct. It's possible for the fulfillment to fail or be rejected but for the authorization to remain active. The two concepts are often treated as one thing, but after a great deal of analysis and discussion (two years worth), FHIR-I landed on them needing to be distinct. The 'Request' resource describes what's authorized. The Task or some other mechanism represents the 'directive'. It's possible for both to be instantiated simultaneously, and there's a lightweight way to do it with a tag if the Request is directed (i.e. it has a specified performer). But the intent does not (and cannot) communicate the 'please do' aspect. That's always separate. In part, because there can be a 'directive' associated with a range of intents - you can have a 'please do' for proposals, plans, orders, placer orders, filler orders, etc. The 'weight' attached to those 'please do' requests can vary based on who's making the request and what the local business rules are. In some environments it's a foregone conclusion that the answer is always yes and it's guaranteed to happen. In others, it's very much up to the recipient whether they're going to do it or not.

### 2. Paul Knapp - 2026-07-08

In a clinical setting, a medical "order" is a specific directive from a licensed healthcare provider (such as a doctor or nurse practitioner) to the medical team. It outlines a patient's plan of care, authorizing treatments like prescriptions, lab work, surgeries, or therapies.

So an 'order' and an 'authorization' are not the same thing. Order speaks to performance of another healthcare person (or perhaps the patient) where typically there or consequences to failure to perform while authorization speaks to permitting a fulfiller to fulfill.

### 10. Lloyd McKenzie - 2026-07-07

Request resources could be plans, proposals, orders, directives, or a variety of other things. Most request intents do not convey authorization. Only order (and to a lesser extent 'directive') imply any level of 'authorization'. There's more detail here which is a section all 'Request' resources are supposed to point to. If someone writes a lab order or a prescription, that's an 'authorization'. In handing it to the patient, there's an implicit request for fulfillment. And that request for fulfillment is transferred to the lab/pharmacy if/when the patient chooses to take it there. But sometimes a DI order is written and the instructions to the patient are "if your ankle isn't feeling better in a few days, go get this done and we'll see if something else is going on. You've got the order/authorization, but the request for fulfillment is contingent and might not happen. (In the same way, the patient might never take the order to a lab or pharmacy.)

### 12. Lloyd McKenzie - 2026-07-06

An order is always just an authorization. The "please fulfill" bit comes from workflow - Task, MessageHeader, operation invocation, a workflow Tag, a fax, a human carrying a piece of paper, etc. A bare Request resource with none of those things cannot ever be assumed to be a solicitation to fulfill. The intent of the Request is "order" or "proposal" or whatever, irrespective of whether there's an associated solicitation for fulfillment
