====

The diagram above shows the Learning Health Ecosystem. As the core of this ecosystem, a Learning Health System (LHS) is one in which science, informatics, incentives, and culture are aligned to enable continuous learning and improvement. In an LHS, data generated from routine care, patient experiences, and health system operations are systematically collected, analyzed, and translated into knowledge that is rapidly fed back into practice. This cyclical process allows the health system to learn from every patient encounter and continuously improves health outcomes, care quality, and efficiency. The diagram depicts this as an ecosystem to highlight the critical role that stakeholders and execution enablers play in the LHS. The process is adaptable to any scale, may begin at any point, and steps that do not apply to a particular process may be skipped. Interoperability across the quadrants (i.e., evidence, knowledge, action, data) is key for data to flow through the ecosystem.
EVIDENCE:

Measurement – By tracking trends and measuring outcomes, organizations can determine if a clinical standard of care functions as intended or identify gaps that necessitate deeper analysis. Measurement acts as both the lens and the engine of learning—ensuring that the progression from data to knowledge is systematic, transparent, and continuously aligned with the goals of better health outcomes, improved patient experience, and more efficient care delivery.

Analysis - Research, public health surveillance, data mining, and other analyses convert data into information, providing a synthesis of existing knowledge on a clinical topic.

KNOWLEDGE:

Clinical practice guidelines – in this step stakeholders, such as professional societies, public health agencies, and governmental bodies, publish current recommended best practices for managing specific diseases and populations. These recommendations and best practices are based on collaboration among clinical subject matter experts, terminologists, informaticists, clinicians and consumers.
Clinical decision support - in this step, the clinical guideline recommendations are translated into clinical decision support (CDS) artifacts to incorporate relevant, evidence-based, and patient-specific clinical recommendations and actions directly within clinical workflow. The structure of clinical support logic is standardized to enable uniform application of clinical recommendations as well as data collection and reporting, and to facilitate measurement and analysis.
ACTION:

Integration - Incorporating recommendations into clinical workflows includes the local implementation of CDS. This approach allows the CDS, an encoded form of the population-level recommendation or best practice, to combine with an individual patient’s data to provide personalized options for that patient’s situation or context, while maintaining standardized logic that represents the clinical recommendation to support data collection and knowledge generation.

Clinical Care - Designed to complement rather than replace clinician judgment, CDS tools provide insights that assist care teams in navigating vast amounts of patient and biomedical data to make informed, high-quality care decisions.To ensure successful implementation, CDS tools include goals to track whether care aligns with guidelines and produces the intended results.

DATA:

Collection –Systematically collected data with standardized metadata ensures consistency across settings, providing the necessary foundation for reporting and analysis.

Reporting – Reporting allows for evaluation and comparison of clinical performance and outcomes internally within healthcare organizations or by payers, or allows for public health agencies at local, regional, national, or global levels to identify trends related to a variety of health issues. The data collected via reporting then serves as part of the evidence base, continuing the cycle of learning enabled by a shared, systematically implemented ecosystem.
